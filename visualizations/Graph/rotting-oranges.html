WHY this is BFS from many starting points at once

Forget code for a second. Put a fire on a dry field. Every burning tile sets its 4 neighbours alight, and that takes exactly one minute. Now light five tiles at the same time. After one minute, every tile touching any of those five is burning. After two minutes, the ring after that. The fire does not take turns — all the fronts advance simultaneously.

That is the whole problem. Rotten oranges are the burning tiles. The question "how many minutes until everything is rotten?" is "how many synchronized waves does the fire need?".

The two ideas that make the code work.
1. Multi-source BFS — seed the queue with every rotten cell before the loop starts. BFS then behaves as if there were one imaginary super-source connected to all of them, so all fronts expand together, for free.
2. 1 minute = 1 BFS level. Instead of storing a time with each cell, you drain the queue a whole ring at a time: for _ in range(len(q)) snapshots how many cells belong to this minute, pops exactly those, and everything they push belongs to the next minute. After the ring is drained, level += 1. That is the clock.
level 0 = cells rotten at the start → level 1 = rotted at minute 1 → level 2 = minute 2 → …

Why len(q) must be read before the pops

Inside the ring you append new cells to the same queue. If you looped "while q" instead, this minute and the next minute would blur into one and level would count nothing meaningful. Freezing len(q) at the top of the ring is what keeps the two generations separated — the queue is really two segments back to back: this minute's ring at the front, next minute's ring behind it. The state panel draws that divider.

Why DFS cannot answer this. DFS dives down one path as deep as it can and only then backtracks. It would happily reach a far orange through a long detour before reaching a near one, so the depth at which it first touches a cell is not the minute that cell rots. You would have to revisit cells with smaller times and re-expand them — that is Dijkstra/BFS logic bolted onto DFS. BFS gets it right on the first touch precisely because it never looks at distance d+1 before finishing all of distance d.

The two exits

while q and fresh > 0 has two ways to stop. Queue empties while fresh oranges remain → those oranges are walled off, answer -1. Or fresh hits 0 → everything rotted, stop immediately even though the queue still holds cells. Those leftover cells have nothing left to infect, so popping them would be pure wasted work. Watch for the callout when that happens.

Complexity: every cell is seeded once, popped once, and looked at by at most 4 neighbours → O(rows × cols) time, O(rows × cols) queue in the worst case.
